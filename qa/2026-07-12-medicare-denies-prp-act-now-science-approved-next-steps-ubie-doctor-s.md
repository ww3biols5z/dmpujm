Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy has surged in popularity for treating joint pain, tendon injuries and certain degenerative conditions. Yet many Medicare patients find their claims denied. This guide explains why Medicare considers PRP "experimental," reviews the scientific data and offers practical next steps—without creating anxiety.

What Is PRP Therapy?

PRP involves drawing a small amount of your blood, spinning it in a centrifuge to concentrate platelets and injecting that platelet-rich mixture into injured tissues. Platelets contain growth factors that may help heal tendons, ligaments, muscles and joints. Common uses include:

Knee osteoarthritis

Tennis elbow

Rotator cuff tendinopathy

Achilles tendinopathy

PRP is generally safe, using your own blood and avoiding steroids. However, its coverage under Medicare remains limited.

Why Medicare Denies PRP for Medicare Patients

Lack of National Coverage Determination (NCD)

Medicare's NCDs outline services considered "reasonable and necessary."

As of now, there is no NCD approving PRP for musculoskeletal or soft-tissue injuries. Without this, PRP is deemed experimental and not covered.

Classification as "Investigational"

CMS (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services) labels PRP investigational due to mixed results in clinical studies.

Investigational treatments generally do not meet coverage criteria.

Insufficient High-Level Evidence

Medicare requires robust, large-scale, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) demonstrating clear benefit.

Many PRP studies are small, use different preparation methods and have short follow-up periods.

Coding and Billing Complexities

PRP injections often use unlisted procedure codes (e.g., CPT 0232T), triggering denials or requests for more information.

Lack of standardized coding makes it harder to secure coverage.

Local Coverage Determinations (LCDs)

Some Medicare Administrative Contractors (MACs) issue LCDs on PRP. Most currently deny it for musculoskeletal conditions.

Even when an LCD allows PRP in limited cases, strict criteria must be met (e.g., refractory to conventional treatments).

The Scientific Reality: What the Research Shows

Medicare's hesitancy rests on the quality and consistency of published data:

Positive Signals

Knee osteoarthritis: Several small RCTs report pain relief and improved function at 6–12 months.

Tendon injuries: Some trials show faster recovery compared to placebo or corticosteroids.

Limitations

Small Sample Sizes: Many studies enroll under 100 patients per group.

Variable Protocols: Differences in platelet concentration, injection frequency and guidance (ultrasound vs. blind).

Short Follow-Up: Most trials track outcomes for less than 1 year.

CMS Requirements

Consistent, multi-site RCTs with standardized PRP preparation.

Long-term safety data beyond 12 months.

Clear patient selection criteria (age, severity, comorbidities).

Until large, well-designed trials address these gaps, Medicare will likely maintain its "experimental" stance.

Your Approved Next Steps

Even if Medicare initially denies PRP, you have options:

Discuss with Your Doctor

Review your diagnosis, previous treatments and overall health.

Ask whether PRP is medically reasonable in your case or if alternatives (e.g., physical therapy, hyaluronic acid injections) might work.

Request a Written "Letter of Medical Necessity"

Your physician can outline why PRP is essential for you, citing your history and any supportive studies.

Submit it with your appeal to Medicare.

File an Appeal

Follow Medicare's five-step appeals process: redetermination, reconsideration, hearing and up to federal court if needed.

Keep organized records: dates of service, provider notes, denial letters and your letter of medical necessity.

Explore Clinical Trials

Consider participating in a registered clinical trial. Costs may be covered by the study sponsor.

Trials help generate the evidence Medicare needs.

Check for Local Coverage Exceptions

Some MACs may allow PRP for specific conditions under narrow criteria.
