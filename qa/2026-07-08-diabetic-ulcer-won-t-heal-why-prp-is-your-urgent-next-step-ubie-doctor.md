Explanation

Why Your Diabetic Foot Ulcer Won't Heal

Chronic foot ulcers in people with diabetes can be stubborn. High blood sugar, poor circulation, nerve damage (neuropathy) and repeated pressure on the same spot all contribute. If an ulcer isn't healing, it's often due to several factors working against your body's natural repair process.

Key Reasons for Poor Healing

Impaired blood flow: Diabetes damages small blood vessels, reducing oxygen and nutrient delivery.

Neuropathy: Loss of sensation means you may not notice minor cuts or pressure points that worsen over time.

High glucose levels: Elevated sugar impairs immune cell function and collagen formation.

Infection risk: A weakened immune system makes it harder to fight off bacteria.

Repetitive trauma: Walking on an open sore delays healing.

If you're dealing with a stubborn ulcer, you're not alone—and there are medical steps you can take.

Standard Care for Diabetic Foot Ulcers

Offloading• Special shoes, casts or braces relieve pressure.• Total contact casts distribute weight evenly.

Debridement• Removal of dead tissue by a trained professional.• Promotes formation of healthy granulation tissue.

Infection control• Topical or oral antibiotics if signs of infection appear.• Frequent dressing changes under sterile conditions.

Blood sugar management• Target A1C usually below 7% (individual goals vary).• Diet, exercise and medications as directed by your doctor.

Moisture balance• Advanced dressings maintain a moist wound bed.• Avoid overly wet or overly dry environments.

When these standard treatments aren't enough, advanced therapies come into play—one of which is platelet-rich plasma (PRP).

What Is PRP and How It Works

PRP is derived from your own blood. A small sample is spun in a centrifuge to concentrate platelets and growth factors. These elements help trigger and accelerate the body's repair mechanisms.

Key Components of PRP

Platelets: Contain growth factors like PDGF, TGF-β and VEGF.

Growth factors: Stimulate new blood vessel formation and collagen production.

Enhance collagen synthesis to rebuild tissue.

Evidence for PRP in Diabetic Foot Ulcers

Clinical studies show promising results:

Faster wound closure compared to standard care alone.

Higher rates of complete healing at earlier time points.

Reduced infection rates in some trials.

Who Might Be a Candidate for PRP?

PRP for diabetic foot ulcers may be considered if:

You've followed offloading, debridement, infection control and glucose management for several weeks.

The ulcer shows minimal reduction in size (often less than 30% improvement in 4 weeks).

You have no contraindications such as blood clotting disorders or active cancer.

You can commit to regular clinic visits for PRP preparation and application.

Your wound care specialist or podiatrist will assess your overall health, ulcer characteristics and treatment history to determine if PRP is right for you.

Monitoring Progress & Next Steps

Photographic documentation: Helps track size, depth and tissue quality.

Measurement: Exact wound dimensions recorded at each visit.

Adjustments: If healing stalls, your team may combine PRP with other advanced therapies (e.g., bioengineered skin substitutes).

If you notice:

Increased redness, swelling or foul odor (signs of infection)

Fever or chills

Final Thoughts & Talking to Your Doctor

PRP for diabetic foot ulcers represents a promising bridge between standard care and more invasive procedures. It won't replace offloading, blood sugar control or proper wound dressings, but it can jump-start stalled healing. Discuss with your physician whether you're a good candidate.

Remember, early intervention is key. Never ignore signs of infection or worsening ulcers. Always speak to a doctor about anything that could be life threatening or serious. Together, you can develop a comprehensive plan to heal your ulcer and protect your long-term foot health.
