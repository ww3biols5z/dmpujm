How I Overcame My Vestibular Migraine: My Personal Journey and Practical Guide

Understanding Vestibular Migraine

Vestibular migraine is a recognized subtype of migraine that features both migraine headaches and vestibular (balance) symptoms. According to Lempert et al. (2012) and Bisdorff et al. (2013):

• Recurrent vestibular symptoms (vertigo, unsteadiness) lasting minutes to hours• History of migraine with or without aura• At least five episodes with one migraine feature (headache, photophobia, phonophobia)• No better explanation by another disorder

Common clinical characteristics:

Women are affected more often than men.

Family history of migraine is common.

Motion sensitivity and specific triggers often play a role.

Understanding these criteria helped me confirm my diagnosis, rule out other causes (like inner-ear infection), and target the right treatments.

Identifying Your Personal Triggers

Each person's triggers can differ, but these are the most common for vestibular migraine:

• Stress and anxiety• Sleep disturbances or jet lag• Hormonal fluctuations (women)• Certain foods and beverages (aged cheese, processed meats, red wine, caffeine)• Sensory overload (bright lights, loud sounds, strong smells)• Dehydration or irregular meals

I kept a daily journal for three months, noting food, sleep hours, stress levels and any migraine or dizzy spells. Patterns emerged:

Skipping breakfast almost always led to afternoon dizziness.

Red wine or strong coffee before bed triggered night-time vertigo.

Late nights and screen fatigue increased head-spin episodes.

Vestibular Rehabilitation and Exercise

Because vestibular migraine affects balance, I worked with a physical therapist on vestibular rehabilitation (VR). Key exercises included:

• Habituation Exercises

Gradual exposure to head movements that provoke mild dizziness (e.g., turning head side to side).

• Gaze Stabilization

Focus on a fixed target while moving the head to train visual-vestibular interaction.

• Balance Training

Standing on foam pads, walking heel-to-toe, closing eyes during stance.

At least 20 minutes of VR exercises, three times per week, sped up my brain's ability to adapt. Over two months, my baseline dizziness decreased significantly.
