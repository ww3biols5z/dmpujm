What Causes Foot Pain and When to See a Doctor

Foot pain is most commonly caused by plantar fasciitis, Achilles tendon problems, stress fractures, arthritis, bunions, or nerve compression. Safe first-line care includes:

Rest and activity modification

Ice applied for 15–20 minutes

Supportive footwear or orthotic inserts

Gentle stretching of the calf and foot

Short-term NSAIDs when appropriate

People with diabetes face higher risks from foot injuries, and Achilles tendon warning signs deserve special attention.

See a podiatrist if pain lasts more than a few days, or if you notice swelling, numbness, tingling, difficulty walking, visible deformity, or a nonhealing wound. Seek urgent care immediately for sudden severe pain, signs of infection, or an injury accompanied by a popping sensation and rapid swelling.

If your discomfort is centered in the back of your heel or lower calf, the Achilles tendon may be involved — and getting clarity early matters. Achilles injuries can progress from mild tendinopathy to a full rupture, and symptoms like stiffness, swelling, or a "pop" should never be ignored. Why Does My Foot Hurt?

Your feet contain 26 bones, over 30 joints, and more than 100 muscles, tendons, and ligaments. That complexity allows you to walk, run, and balance — but it also means many things can go wrong.

Some of the most common causes of foot pain include:

1. Plantar Fasciitis

Sharp pain in the heel, especially with your first steps in the morning

Often worse after long periods of sitting

Caused by inflammation of the thick band of tissue along the bottom of the foot

2. Achilles Tendon Problems

Pain or stiffness in the back of the heel

Worse with walking uphill or climbing stairs

When Is It Time to See a Podiatrist?

Not every sore foot needs medical attention. But certain symptoms shouldn't be ignored.

You should consider seeing a podiatrist if you have:

Pain lasting more than a few days despite rest

Swelling that doesn't improve

Difficulty walking or bearing weight

Numbness or tingling

Visible deformity

Signs of infection (redness, warmth, pus, fever)

A wound that isn't healing — especially if you have diabetes

If you experience sudden, severe pain after an injury or hear a "pop" followed by swelling and bruising, seek medical care promptly. That could signal a tendon rupture or fracture.

And if you ever notice chest pain, shortness of breath, or leg swelling with calf pain, seek emergency care immediately — these could indicate serious conditions like blood clots.

What Does a Podiatrist Actually Do?

A podiatrist is a doctor specializing in foot and ankle health. They are trained to:

Diagnose structural, muscular, and nerve problems

Order and interpret imaging like X-rays or MRIs

Provide custom orthotics

Prescribe medications

Perform minor procedures or surgery when necessary

Help prevent complications in chronic conditions like diabetes

What Happens at a Podiatrist Appointment?

A visit is usually straightforward and focused.

Your podiatrist may:

Ask about your symptoms and activity level

Examine your foot structure and walking pattern

Test range of motion and strength

Order imaging if needed

Recommend physical therapy

Prescribe orthotics

Suggest injections for inflammation

Discuss surgical options only if conservative treatments fail

Most foot conditions improve without surgery when treated early.

Don't Ignore Persistent Foot Pain

It's easy to downplay foot pain. Many people assume it's just part of aging or being active. But untreated problems can:

Change how you walk

Lead to knee, hip, or back pain

Reduce mobility

Increase fall risk

Become harder to treat over time

Early evaluation by a podiatrist often leads to simpler treatment and faster recovery.
