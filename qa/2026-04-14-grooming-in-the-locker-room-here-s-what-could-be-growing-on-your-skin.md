# ​Grooming in the Locker Room? Here’s What Could Be Growing on Your Skin

You finish a workout, and the locker room is where you put yourself back together again. You shower. You shave. You avail yourself of the rather nice products provided by your rather expensive gym. But the place that’s meant to leave you feeling clean and handsome could leave you, gulp, infected by bacteria. (Maybe that's why it ranks among the germiest places to have sex.) Here's the expert advice on three invisible enemies that lurk in the locker room, and how to avoid them during your grooming routine.

While You're Showering... Athlete's Foot

"The locker room is a breeding ground for bacteria and other microbes," says Joshua Zeichner Director of Cosmetic and Clinical Research in Dermatology at Mount Sinai Hospital in NYC.

Think of the sweat from your workout. The dirt and oil on your skin. Now multiply that by all the guys who traipse through the locker room, showering off and leaving filth behind. Bacteria and viruses thrive on that stuff—no matter how fancy your gym is.

And where does most of the grime end up? On the floor of the shower, and being tracked around the room. Time to talk about tinea pedis, the fungal infection affectionately known as "athlete's foot."

"Tinea pedis is an infection of fungus into the superficial layers of skin on the feet and in between the toes," says Zeichner. "Typically you'll see white, moist skin that is falling apart."

Best way to avoid it? Wear flip-flops in the shower like you did in college. Barring that, dry your feet thoroughly afterward—or use anti-fungal powders either on your feet or in your socks. Zeichner also recommends using an over-the-counter anti-fungal cream like Lotrimin from time to time as a prophylactic.

Need another reason to watch out? Besides being disgusting, a case of athlete's foot can hasten the development of fungal nail infections. In Zeichner's words, athlete's foot creates "a reservoir of fungus that can spread to the nails." (For more on toenail care, read this.)

When your nails are infected, over-the-counter treatments aren't going to be as effective. So act early and keep those feet clean.

While You're Shaving... Staph

"Staph." It sounds serious. It sounds vague. It sounds scary enough that it's probably confined to hospitals, right? No. We already know it can live up to three days on your workout equipment. And in the locker room, it can be part of your shave.

"If you're shaving in the locker room and get nicks or cuts, that increases your chance of getting an infection," says Zeichner. "That's true anywhere, but especially in the locker room where there tends to be a prevalence of bacteria."

A full-on staph infection usually involves a pus-filled abscess deep in your skin. But staph has a little cousin. His name is folliculitis.

Folliculitis affects the hair follicles. It's kind of like razor bumps. Until you realize each one is a miniature infection. Filled with pus.

This one's bacterial, so prevent it with antibacterial products. Using antibacterial soap in the shower should be enough. If worried about your face, Zeichner recommends adding a scrub to your gym bag such as the Neutrogena Clear Pore Daily Scrub, which contains bacteria-killing benzoyl peroxide.

While You're Changing... Ringworm

It's not a worm. And it's not always ring-shaped. But if you have a fungal infection somewhere besides your feet, it could be ringworm.

"It looks like a circular rash with a scale on it," says Zeichner, "and it loves wet, humid environments, wet towels, the gym mat, a padded bench—things that are soft."

Typically, you'll see this one on the torso, upper arms or thighs, but it can be anywhere. Jock itch? That's another name for the same thing.

These fungal rashes thrive wherever skin is warm, damp and in the dark. So when you get changed, be thorough. Bring fresh clothes—don't forget underwear. Don't spend all day in the shorts from your morning run.

The "good news"—if that phrase applies to anything with "worm" in the name—is that you can treat this one over-the-counter. Reach for the same anti-fungal creams you'd use on feet, like Lotrimin or Lamisil.

And, as always, know when to seek help. "If it's not improving," says Zeichner, "you need to see a dermatologist to get it checked."
