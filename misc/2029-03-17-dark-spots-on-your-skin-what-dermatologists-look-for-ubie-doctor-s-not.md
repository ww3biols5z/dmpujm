Dark spots on the skin—also called hyperpigmentation—can develop from sun exposure, aging, hormonal shifts, inflammation, or underlying health conditions. While most dark spots are harmless, certain changes in size, shape, color, or texture may signal a need for medical evaluation. Dermatologists typically assess a spot's history, appearance, and pattern using tools such as dermoscopy, Wood's lamp examination, or biopsy, and may recommend treatments ranging from topical therapies to in-office procedures like chemical peels or laser therapy.

Why Dark Spots on Skin Develop

Understanding the cause of dark spots on skin guides effective treatment. Common reasons include:

Sun exposure• Ultraviolet (UV) radiation stimulates melanin production, leading to sunspots (solar lentigines).

Aging• "Age spots" accumulate over time, especially on sun-exposed areas like the face, hands and shoulders.

Hormonal changes• Melasma often appears in pregnant women or with birth control use; it produces brown-gray patches on the cheeks, forehead and upper lip.

Skin injury or inflammation• Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation follows acne, burns, cuts or eczema.

Medications• Certain antibiotics, chemotherapy drugs and hormone therapies can trigger pigmentation changes.

Underlying medical conditions• Addison's disease, hemochromatosis or liver disorders may cause diffuse or localized darkening.

What Dermatologists Look For

When you see a dermatologist about dark spots on skin, they will assess:

History and Timing

Onset: sudden vs. gradual

Triggers: sun exposure, new medications, hormonal events

Symptoms: itching, tenderness, bleeding

Physical Examination• Size, shape and color variation• Borders: well-defined vs. irregular• Distribution: localized vs. widespread• Surface changes: scale, crust, ulceration

Pattern Recognition

Freckles: small, uniform tan spots that become darker with sun exposure

Lentigines: larger, brown spots that remain year-round

Seborrheic keratoses: waxy, "stuck-on" appearance

Melasma: symmetric patches on the cheeks, forehead, upper lip

Melanoma Warning Signs (ABCDEs)• Asymmetry: one half doesn't match the other• Border: irregular, ragged edges• Color: multiple shades (brown, black, red)• Diameter: larger than 6 mm (about a pencil eraser)• Evolving: changes in size, shape or color

Diagnostic Tools

Dermoscopy: magnified view to spot atypical features

Wood's lamp: ultraviolet light reveals deeper pigmentation

Skin biopsy: definitive diagnosis when malignancy or unusual patterns are suspected

Common Conditions and Dark Spots

Dermatologists categorize dark spots into benign, pre-malignant and malignant groups:

• Benign Hyperpigmentation

Solar lentigines ("sunspots")

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation

Melasma

• Pre-malignant Lesions

Actinic keratoses: rough, scaly patches that may progress to squamous cell carcinoma

• Malignant Lesions

Melanoma: the most dangerous skin cancer, requires prompt detection

Basal cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma can also present with pigmentation

Treatment Options for Dark Spots on Skin

Treatment depends on the type, location and depth of pigmentation, as well as your skin type and medical history.

Topical Therapies

Hydroquinone• A melanin-inhibiting agent, often considered the gold standard.

Retinoids (tretinoin, adapalene)• Speed up cell turnover, promoting even pigment distribution.

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid)• An antioxidant that brightens skin and neutralizes free radicals.

Azelaic acid• Reduces melanin synthesis and has anti-inflammatory effects.

Kojic acid, niacinamide, licorice extract• Natural extracts that can lighten pigmentation.

In-Office Procedures

Chemical peels• Glycolic, salicylic or trichloroacetic acid peels remove superficial pigmented cells.

Microdermabrasion• Gentle skin polishing to reduce light spots.

Laser and intense pulsed light (IPL)• Target melanin deep in the skin; best performed by experienced providers.

Microneedling• Promotes collagen remodeling and pigment dispersion.

Combination Therapy

Often, dermatologists pair topical agents with in-office procedures for better results.

Maintenance with daily sunscreen and home care is essential to prevent recurrence.

Prevention Strategies

Preventing new dark spots is as important as treating existing ones:

Apply broad-spectrum sunscreen (SPF 30 or higher) daily, even on cloudy days.

Reapply sunscreen every two hours when outdoors or after swimming/sweating.

Wear wide-brimmed hats, sunglasses and UV-protective clothing.

Avoid peak sun hours (10 a.m. to 4 p.m.) when UV rays are strongest.

Use gentle skincare products; avoid harsh scrubs or abrasive treatments on pigmented areas.

When to Seek Medical Advice

Most dark spots on skin are harmless, but you should see a dermatologist if you notice:

A spot changing in size, shape or color

New dark spots appearing after age 30 without obvious triggers

Spots that itch, bleed or fail to heal

A cluster of pigmented lesions with irregular borders

Underlying Health Concerns

Certain patterns of hyperpigmentation could reflect systemic issues:

Addison's disease: diffuse bronzing of the skin, often accompanied by fatigue and low blood pressure

Hemochromatosis: bronze or gray discoloration, joint pain and liver dysfunction

Hormonal imbalances: thyroid disorders, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and adrenal disorders

A thorough medical history and blood tests may be recommended if a systemic cause is suspected.

Talking to Your Dermatologist

Prepare for your appointment to maximize the benefit:

List any new or changing spots, including when they appeared.

Note any medications, supplements or topical products you use.

Bring photos to show changes over time.

Be ready to discuss family history of skin cancer or pigmentation disorders.
