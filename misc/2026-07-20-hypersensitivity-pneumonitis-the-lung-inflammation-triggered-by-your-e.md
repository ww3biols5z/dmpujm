Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) is an immune-mediated lung inflammation caused by inhaling airborne particles like mold, bird proteins, bacteria, or chemicals in homes, workplaces, or hobby settings. Symptoms can appear within hours of exposure—fever, cough, and shortness of breath—or develop slowly over months, potentially leading to chronic lung scarring. Explanation

What Is Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis?

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), sometimes called "extrinsic allergic alveolitis," is a lung inflammation caused by inhaling tiny particles—often mold, bacteria, animal proteins or chemicals—in your home, workplace or hobbies. When your immune system overreacts to these airborne substances (called antigens), it triggers inflammation in the small air sacs (alveoli) of your lungs.

Key points:

Not an infection, but an immune response.

Can affect anyone repeatedly exposed to certain environmental triggers.

Ranges from mild and reversible to severe and chronic lung damage if untreated.

Common Environmental Triggers

HP can be caused by a variety of antigens found in everyday environments. Knowing these sources helps you track down the root cause and prevent further lung injury.

• Farmer's lung: moldy hay, straw or grain• Bird fancier's lung: proteins from feathers or droppings (pigeons, parrots)• Humidifier or "hot tub" lung: bacteria and amoebae in poorly maintained water systems• Mushroom worker's lung: spores from growing fungi• Humidifier lung: contaminated vaporizers, HVAC systems• Chemical worker's lung: isocyanates used in plastics, paints, adhesives• Water-damaged buildings: mold growth in walls, carpets, air ducts

How Is HP Diagnosed?

Diagnosing hypersensitivity pneumonitis is a step-by-step process aimed at confirming lung inflammation and identifying the offending antigen.

Imaging Tests

High-resolution CT (HRCT) scan: shows ground-glass opacities or fibrotic changes

Chest X-ray: may reveal hazy spots or nodules

Pulmonary Function Tests (PFTs)

Measure lung volumes, airflow and gas exchange

Often show a restrictive pattern (reduced lung capacity) and low oxygen levels

Blood Tests

Look for antibodies (precipitins) against suspected antigens

Elevated inflammatory markers

Bronchoalveolar Lavage (BAL)

Washes out cells from small airways; high levels of lymphocytes support HP diagnosis

Lung Biopsy (if needed)

Obtained via bronchoscopy or surgery

Confirms inflammation and fibrosis

Treatment Strategies

The cornerstone of HP treatment is removing or reducing exposure to the triggering antigen. Additional therapies may include:

• Antigen avoidance

Relocate birds, clean or replace humidifiers, improve ventilation

Use masks or respirators when avoidance isn't possible

• Medications

Corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone) to reduce lung inflammation

Immunosuppressants (in chronic cases) under specialist supervision

• Supportive Care

Oxygen therapy if blood oxygen levels remain low

Pulmonary rehabilitation: guided exercise, breathing techniques

• Monitoring

Regular lung function tests and imaging to track improvement or progression

Ongoing evaluation by a pulmonologist or occupational medicine expert

Preventing Hypersensitivity Pneumonitis

Once you know your trigger, you can take steps to protect your health:

• Maintain clean, dry living and working environments.• Control humidity (keep indoor humidity below 50%).• Clean or replace filters in HVAC systems regularly.• Wear appropriate protective gear (masks, respirators) when handling dusty, moldy or chemical-laden materials.• Educate family members or coworkers about potential risks.

Final Thoughts

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis is a treatable but potentially serious lung condition triggered by environmental antigens. Identifying and eliminating the source of exposure is as important as medical therapy. Early diagnosis, avoidance measures and close follow-up with a healthcare provider can help you regain lung function and prevent long-term complications.

If you have any life-threatening or serious symptoms—such as severe shortness of breath, chest pain or high fever—seek immediate medical attention. For ongoing concerns, always speak to a doctor or pulmonologist to guide you through diagnosis and management.
