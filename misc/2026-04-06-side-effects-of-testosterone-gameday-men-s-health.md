# Side Effects of Testosterone | Gameday Men’s Health

Reviewed by: Dr. Hal and Gameday's Medical Advisory Board

Testosterone plays a crucial role in almost every aspect of men’s health. Testosterone can influence energy levels, muscle mass, fat distribution, mood, libido, bone density, and cognitive function. When testosterone levels are healthy, many men feel stronger, sharper, and more resilient. However, when levels decline, which often happens gradually with age, symptoms like fatigue, low motivation, reduced sex drive, and loss of muscle can start to interfere with daily life. (1)

Testosterone therapy options like TRT can be highly beneficial for men who have low testosterone and testosterone deficiency symptoms confirmed by proper testing and who are treated under medical supervision. (2) With the right treatment, many men experience meaningful improvements in quality of life, confidence, and their overall well being. At the same time, testosterone is a powerful hormone, and changing hormone levels can come with side effects that men need to be aware of.

Why Testosterone Can Cause Side Effects

Testosterone does not operate in isolation. It is part of a tightly regulated hormonal system that relies on feedback loops between the brain, pituitary gland, and testes. (3) When testosterone is introduced from an outside source, the body may adjust its own production and signaling in response. These adjustments are often what drive side effects.

Several factors influence whether side effects occur and how noticeable they are. Dosage levels are one of the most important factors. A dose that is too high for an individual can push testosterone levels beyond the healthy range, increasing the likelihood of unwanted effects of overly high testosterone levels. Delivery method also plays a role, since injections, topical treatments, pellets, and oral options are absorbed differently and affect hormone levels in distinct patterns.Beyond dosing and delivery methods, each individual patient will react differently. Genetics, body composition, liver function, cardiovascular health, and existing medical conditions all play a role in determining how the body processes testosterone. This is why baseline testing is essential before starting therapy. Measuring testosterone, red blood cell levels, cholesterol, and other markers creates a reference point that helps clinicians tailor treatment and identify changes early.

Common Short-Term Side Effects

Many side effects associated with testosterone therapy appear early, often within the first few weeks or months of starting treatment. These are typically dose-related and can often improve with adjustments from your clinician.

One common short-term effect is acne or oily skin. Testosterone can increase oil production in the skin, which may lead to breakouts, particularly on the face, chest, or back. This tends to be more noticeable in men who are already prone to acne. (4)

Fluid retention is another potential issue. Some men notice mild swelling in the ankles or hands as the body retains more water. While usually temporary, significant swelling should be discussed with a clinician.

Mood changes can also occur when testosterone is increased. Testosterone can influence neurotransmitters in the brain, which may lead to irritability, restlessness, or feeling more emotionally reactive. (5) Both low and high levels of testosterone can lead to changes in mood. In many cases, stabilizing hormone levels reduces these effects over time.

Increased appetite is sometimes reported, particularly in men who gain muscle mass or become more physically active after starting therapy. While this can support positive body composition changes, it is important to maintain balanced nutrition.

Potential Long-Term Side Effects

Long-term side effects are less common when testosterone therapy is properly managed, but they are still important to understand before starting treatment.

One of the most closely monitored changes involves red blood cell levels. Testosterone can stimulate red blood cell production, which may lead to elevated hematocrit levels. (6) If levels rise too high, blood can become thicker, increasing strain on the cardiovascular system. Regular blood testing helps identify this issue early, and adjustments or therapeutic blood donation can reduce risk.

Hormonal suppression of natural testosterone production is a common physiological response to therapy. When external testosterone is introduced, the body may reduce or stop its own production. This is an expected physiological response, not a complication, but it is important for men to understand that stopping therapy abruptly can lead to symptoms if natural production has been suppressed.

For men planning on having children, fertility considerations are also worth monitoring. Testosterone therapy can sometimes reduce the production of sperm by signaling the body to slow or stop testicular testosterone production. While this doesn't mean permanent fertility loss, it does mean that intentional strategies for preserving fertility may be needed.

Prostate-related monitoring is also part of long-term care when it comes to testosterone therapy. Testosterone does not cause prostate cancer, but it can increase PSA levels. Monitoring prostate-specific antigen levels and urinary symptoms helps ensure prostate health remains stable over time.

Sexual and Reproductive Side Effects

Testosterone has a direct impact on the reproductive system, which is why some side effects of testosterone therapy often occur in this area.

Testicular shrinkage can happen when the testes reduce their own testosterone production in response to therapy. This change is usually gradual and varies from person to person. (7) Some men notice a change, while others do not.

Changes in sperm production are closely tied to fertility. As external testosterone increases, signals from the brain that stimulate sperm production may decrease. Men who want to maintain their fertility should discuss options with their clinician before starting therapy.When testosterone levels adjust, libido can fluctuate. Increased testosterone can cause many men to experience enhanced sex drive, while others notice temporary changes in their overall sexual desire while levels are stabilizing. In these instances, a fine tuning in treatment can help the issue resolve itself and reset libido to a healthy level.

Testosterone also plays a key role in cardiovascular and metabolic health. During testosterone therapy, regular monitoring of blood pressure, cholesterol, and other metabolic health concerns is critical.

Blood pressure may change during therapy, particularly if fluid retention occurs or if there is elevated hematocrit caused by increased red blood cell production. (8) Men with preexisting cardiovascular disease or risk factors are more likely to experience issues when treatment starts.

Cholesterol levels can also shift with therapy. Testosterone may influence levels of HDL and LDL cholesterol, though responses vary. Factors That Influence Side Effects

Not all men experience side effects in the same way, and several factors shape the risk levels for each individual.

TRT dosage and delivery method are primary factors that determine how side effects may present. High doses increase the likelihood of side effects, especially when levels rise above the normal physiological range. Additionally, injectable testosterone can lead to peaks and valleys in hormone levels, while topical treatments may provide steadier absorption.

Age and overall health play a role as well. Older men or those with underlying conditions may metabolize testosterone differently and require closer monitoring. Lifestyle factors like sleep quality, alcohol intake, stress level, and physical activity can also influence how the body responds to hormone therapy. (9)

How Side Effects Are Monitored and Managed

Managing side effects starts with proactive monitoring, and regular blood testing is the foundation of safe testosterone therapy. These tests track testosterone levels, red blood cell counts, cholesterol, and other key markers.

Clinicians can use dose adjustments, such as splitting or lowering doses to resolve small side effects. Larger issues may require more significant changes such as alternative therapies or stopping the treatment altogether.

For specific instances such as fertility preservation or estrogen management, doctors may seek out alternatives to traditional testosterone therapies like TRT such as Clomid, enclomiphene, or hCG injections. (10)

Testosterone therapy should always be guided by a medical professional who understands hormone physiology and the individual patient’s needs. Self-directed adjustments increase the risk of complications and should always be avoided.

When to Talk to a Medical Provider

Open communication with a provider is essential throughout the testosterone therapy treatment process. Serious symptoms, like persistent swelling, shortness of breath, severe mood changes, or significant changes in urination, require immediate medical attention and shouldn’t be ignored.

Proactive communication helps prevent minor side effects from becoming major problems. It’s also critical to remember that changing doses or schedules without guidance can disrupt hormone balance and substantially increase side effects.
