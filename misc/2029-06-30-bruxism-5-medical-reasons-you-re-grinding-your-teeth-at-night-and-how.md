Chronic teeth grinding (bruxism) is usually a symptom of an underlying medical condition, not a standalone problem. Identifying the root cause is essential for effective treatment.

Common causes of chronic teeth grinding:

Stress and anxiety — triggers involuntary jaw clenching, especially during sleep

Sleep apnea — grinding occurs as the body reacts to disrupted breathing

Neurological disorders — such as Parkinson's disease or other movement disorders

Certain medications — including SSRIs and stimulants

Acid reflux (GERD) — nighttime irritation can trigger grinding

How doctors treat bruxism: Treatment targets the underlying cause—stress management, CPAP therapy or oral appliances for sleep apnea, medication adjustments, and custom dental night guards to prevent tooth damage.

Explanation

Teeth grinding, or bruxism, affects up to 30% of adults and can lead to jaw pain, headaches, worn enamel and disrupted sleep. While occasional grinding may seem harmless, chronic bruxism often signals an underlying medical issue. Below are five common medical reasons for nighttime teeth grinding and how doctors typically address each cause.

1. Stress and Anxiety Disorders

High emotional stress or an anxiety disorder is one of the most frequent triggers for bruxism. When you feel tense, your jaw muscles may clench unconsciously during sleep.

Activation of the "fight-or-flight" response increases muscle tension.

Racing thoughts make deep, restful sleep harder to achieve.

Morning jaw soreness or tension.

Headaches upon waking.

Teeth that feel flattened or chipped.

Stress management techniques: guided relaxation, mindfulness meditation or biofeedback.

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT): targets anxious thought patterns that contribute to muscle tension.

Referral to a mental-health professional: when an anxiety disorder is diagnosed, talk therapy or medication may be recommended.

2. Sleep-Related Breathing Disorders (Obstructive Sleep Apnea)

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) causes repeated breathing pauses and low oxygen levels overnight. The body reacts by briefly waking you up, which often triggers jaw clenching and teeth grinding.

Airway blockage leads to micro-arousals and surges in muscle activity.

The clenching reflex may help reopen the airway.

Loud snoring or gasping for air at night.

Excessive daytime sleepiness.

Dry mouth or sore throat upon waking.

Polysomnography (sleep study): confirms OSA and measures severity.

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP): a mask that delivers gentle air pressure to keep the airway open.

Oral appliances: custom devices that reposition the jaw to improve airflow.

Lifestyle changes: weight loss, sleeping on your side and avoiding alcohol before bed.

3. Neurological Conditions

Certain neurological disorders can interfere with normal movement control, leading to involuntary jaw muscle contractions and grinding.

Parkinson's disease

Huntington's disease

Dystonia (involuntary muscle spasms)

Dysfunction in brain regions that regulate muscle tone.

Medication regimens for these diseases may also affect sleep architecture.

Neurological evaluation: scans or clinical exams to pinpoint the underlying disorder.

Medication adjustments: changing dosages or types to minimize side effects.

Botulinum toxin (Botox) injections: selectively weaken overactive jaw muscles to reduce grinding.

Coordinated care: collaboration between neurologists, dentists and sleep specialists.

4. Medication Side Effects

Some prescription drugs, especially those acting on the central nervous system, list bruxism as a potential side effect.

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)

Antipsychotics

Stimulants (for ADHD)

Alterations in neurotransmitters such as dopamine and serotonin can increase jaw muscle activity.

Grinding that begins after starting or increasing a medication dose.

New onset of jaw pain or enamel wear soon after a medication change.

Medication review: assess risk versus benefit of the current regimen.

Dose adjustment: lowering the dose may reduce bruxism without compromising treatment.

Switching medications: choosing alternative drugs with a lower risk of teeth grinding.

Adjunct therapies: mouthguards or stress-reduction techniques to protect your teeth while adjusting medications.

5. Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)

GERD occurs when stomach acid flows back into the esophagus, irritating the lining and sometimes triggering bruxism.

Acid exposure stimulates nerves that may reflexively activate jaw muscles.

Nighttime reflux episodes can fragment sleep, leading to clenching.

Heartburn or chest discomfort at night.

Acid taste in the mouth upon waking.

Frequent throat clearing or chronic cough.

Acid-suppressive therapy: proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) or H2 blockers.

Elevate the head of the bed.

Avoid late-night meals and caffeine.

Dental guards: protect teeth during reflux-related arousals.

Monitoring: follow-up to ensure reflux is controlled and grinding improves.

How Doctors Diagnose and Treat Bruxism

Effective management of teeth grinding bruxism typically involves a multi-step approach:

Clinical Assessment

Review medical history, medication list and sleep patterns.

Examine teeth, jaw muscles and bite alignment.

Diagnostic Tests

Sleep study (polysomnography) to detect sleep apnea or other disorders.

Imaging (X-rays or MRI) if neurological causes are suspected.

Protective Dental Devices

Custom nightguards or occlusal splints cushion teeth and redistribute pressure.

Over-the-counter guards can help short term but may not fit as well.

Behavioral Therapies

Stress-reduction programs (biofeedback, relaxation exercises).

Habit-reversal training: becoming aware of daytime clenching and intentionally relaxing the jaw.

Medical Treatments

Muscle relaxants or low-dose antidepressants prescribed short-term.

Botox injections into jaw muscles in resistant cases.

Treat Underlying Conditions

CPAP for sleep apnea.

Medication changes if drug-induced bruxism is suspected.

GERD therapy to reduce acid reflux.
