# Ruston Kelly's Road to Recovery

Nashville-based musician RUSTON KELLY, 32, has often written himself into his songs as characters who are racing toward rock bottom. And he lived it: Between rehab stints, "I was buying shit at CVS to mix with other shit to get high" and coming down and feeling like "walking death." Now he’s going through a personal and musical remix.

MH: When was the first time you realized you had a problem when it came to substance abuse?

RK: I was dating this girl at the time, and she had a bottle of pills. I hadn't really played with many drugs before. I was like 20 years old. So I took one, but right before I took it, I had this really weird feeling: "If I take this, I'll never stop taking this."

It was amphetamine salts, which a variety of people prescribed low dosages for ADD and ADHD. That single pill led into handfuls of bills, which led into drinking heavily which led to cocaine, which led to opiates. Everything, just from that one moment. But it wasn't until I got really sick that I was like, "Yeah, I got a problem."

How did being a touring musician factor into it?

I felt encouraged by whatever it was that I was doing, which is really dangerous. To be half drunk on a barstool playing for like two hours and people getting down to that and complimenting you so much on what you’re doing. I felt like, "Maybe I just understand myself as an artist better when I don’t understand who I am as a person."

What would you say was rock bottom?

Rock bottom was when I would be buying shit at CVS to mix with other shit to get high. That high is A) not good. And B) is the worst come down. It's worse than wanting to kill yourself. It's just like you're walking death. You're dead, man. You got nothing to say and you don't care about anything, but yet you care about everything. You care so much, but your intent towards the good things in your life is shriveled. It's nothing and you're nothing. That was rock bottom.

Your recovery journey speaks to the idea that recovery is not a linear path.

I relapsed more times than I can count. In this period of relapses, I went to a manual labor rehab where they gave you a shovel and a Bible. It's like, "Sweat and pray it out." I found more of a Zen way of prayer in doing the labor. In fact, the chorus for "Paratrooper's Battlecry" was written while I was mowing some old lady's grass. I did all that and I felt like this sense of being saved.

Two weeks later, I had a handful of dexedrine and a bottle of Jack Daniel's. I relapsed on and off for six or so years.

How does going back into your past—playing songs from that time, talking about it with people—factor into your recovery?

You have to know where you've been to know where you're going.

Jason Isbell has been such a such a helpful friend to me whether he really knows it or not. He told me this concept that David Crosby told him which is called "slip dreams." I texted him and I was like, "Dude, why is it that I'm in the happiest place, and most surefooted place my life, but I woke up dreaming of heroin?" And he's like, "Play out that fantasy. Think about how it would feel knowing it would affect your family. Think about how it would feel in the morning, just that sickness."

That doesn't mean I'm about to relapse; it means that I'm an addict. Jason has been a source of saying, "Look, man, you're not as crazy as you think. You're actually a lot stronger than you think."

How do you think recovery has made you stronger?

It's a deep human study in resilience and willpower. We apply both those things to a variety of different elements and situations in our life. When you can pull out, you have a more heightened understanding of and a deeper sense of value. You look at value a lot differently.

You know what it's like to not have really simple things that seem to be building the very essence of being a happy and fulfilled person. You know what it's like for your cup to be empty and you keep scraping the bottom. You know what it's like to feel ugly all the time.
