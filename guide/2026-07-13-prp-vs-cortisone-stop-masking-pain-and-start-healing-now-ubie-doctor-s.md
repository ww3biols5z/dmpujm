PRP vs Cortisone Injection: Why One Heals and the Other Masks Pain

When you're dealing with joint pain, tendon issues or arthritis, two common treatments are Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) and cortisone injections. Both can bring relief, but they work in very different ways. Understanding how each one functions—and their benefits and drawbacks—can help you make an informed decision for your health.

What Is a PRP Injection?

PRP stands for Platelet-Rich Plasma. It's an autologous treatment, meaning your own blood is used:

A small sample of your blood is drawn.

The sample is spun in a centrifuge to concentrate the platelets.

The platelet-rich portion is injected into the injured area.

Platelets are rich in growth factors that can:

Stimulate tissue repair

Encourage new blood vessel formation

Modulate inflammation

PRP is often used for:

Tendon injuries (e.g., tennis elbow, Achilles tendinopathy)

Ligament sprains

Mild to moderate osteoarthritis

Muscle strains

Because PRP harnesses your body's natural healing ability, its goal is to repair and regenerate tissue over time.

What Is a Cortisone Injection?

Cortisone (a corticosteroid) is a potent anti-inflammatory medication. During a cortisone injection:

A synthetic steroid is mixed with a local anesthetic.

The mixture is injected into the joint or soft tissue.

Cortisone works by:

Suppressing immune cells that drive inflammation

Reducing pain signals in the area

It's most commonly used for:

Acute flare-ups of arthritis

Bursitis and tendonitis

Joint swelling

Cortisone injections are designed to mask pain and decrease inflammation quickly, rather than promote long-term tissue repair.

Benefits of PRP Injections

Natural Healing: Uses your own platelets—no foreign chemicals.

Longer-Lasting: Studies show symptom relief can last 6–12 months or more.

Fewer Repeated Shots: Often just one to three treatments needed.

Low Risk of Immunogenic Reaction: Because it's derived from your own blood.

Potential to Slow Disease Progression: By promoting tissue regeneration in osteoarthritis.

Credible research highlights include:

A 2020 meta-analysis in the American Journal of Sports Medicine found PRP superior to placebo for tendon injuries.

Multiple randomized trials have shown improved cartilage quality in mild osteoarthritis over 6–12 months.

Clinical Scenarios: When to Choose PRP vs Cortisone

Chronic Tendon or Ligament Injuries

PRP: Promotes collagen formation and tissue remodeling.

Cortisone: May provide quick relief but risk of tendon rupture if overused.

Mild to Moderate Osteoarthritis

PRP: Can slow cartilage degradation and improve joint function long term.

Cortisone: Helpful for acute flares; consider limiting to 2–3 injections per year.

Acute Inflammatory Flares

PRP: Not ideal if pain is suddenly severe; healing response takes weeks.

Cortisone: Offers rapid reduction in swelling and pain to restore mobility.

Final Thoughts

PRP is a regenerative therapy that aims to heal tissues over time.

Your choice depends on whether you need fast relief or long-term recovery.
